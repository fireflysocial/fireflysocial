# The Science of Friendship

Research on how friendships form, and what it means for Firefly Social's design. The tables distinguish study findings from proposed product implications. They include original studies, reviews, and public reports. Association is not proof of causation; none of these sources validates Firefly's particular workflow or deadlines.

## Findings

### 1. Time and repetition

| Finding | Source | Implication for Firefly Social |
|---------|--------|----------------------|
| Hall estimated roughly **40–60 hours** to casual friendship, **80–100 hours** to friendship, and **200+ hours** to close friendship in the studied samples. These are estimates from self-reports, not universal thresholds. Leisure time and some kinds of everyday talk were associated with closeness; work time alone was not a reliable substitute. | Hall, J. A. (2019). How many hours does it take to make a friend? *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 36*(4), 1278–1296. [doi:10.1177/0265407518761225](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407518761225) · [KU summary](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend) · [Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_long_does_it_take_to_make_a_friend) | A single event is unlikely to provide the sustained contact described in the study. Friendship progress is not linear, so a two-hour event cannot be called "4% of a friendship." Track estimated shared event time as an exposure metric (the north star is re-meeting within 21 days; see [v1-user-flow.md](../workflow/v1-user-flow.md#key-metrics-per-step)). Prefer recurring events and social (not task-focused) formats. |
| **Physical proximity predicts friendship.** In MIT student housing, residents were far more likely to befriend next-door neighbours than people a few doors away. | Festinger, L., Schachter, S., & Back, K. (1950). *Social Pressures in Informal Groups: A Study of Human Factors in Housing.* Harper. | Firefly Social should create **proximity by design**: put the same people in the same place repeatedly (same pod, same recurring event). |
| **Random seat neighbours** at a one-off introductory session were more likely to be friends **one year later**. Even sitting in the same row had an effect. | Back, M. D., Schmukle, S. C., & Egloff, B. (2008). Becoming friends by chance. *Psychological Science, 19*(5), 439–440. [doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02106.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02106.x) · [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2008/06/080602163842.htm) | Small, assigned first contacts (a pod, a table) can have lasting effects. **Assigning people to a pod is not artificial; it's how many real friendships start.** |
| **Mere exposure:** four female confederates attended a class different numbers of times without interacting with students. Greater exposure increased students’ attraction and perceived similarity ratings. | Moreland, R. L., & Beach, S. R. (1992). Exposure effects in the classroom. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 28*(3), 255–276. [doi:10.1016/0022-1031(92)90055-O](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031(92)90055-O) | Test whether repeated attendance helps familiarity in this setting; the classroom result does not guarantee friendship. Recommend **the same series** to connections, not just any event. |
| **Familiarity increases attraction in live interaction.** More conversation between strangers raised liking, rebutting the claim that familiarity breeds contempt. | Reis, H. T., Maniaci, M. R., Caprariello, P. A., Eastwick, P. W., & Finkel, E. J. (2011). Familiarity does indeed promote attraction in live interaction. *JPSP, 101*(3), 557–570. [doi:10.1037/a0022885](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0022885) · [PDF](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/psy/people/faculty/reis_harry/assets/pdf/ReisManiaciCaprarielloEastwickFinkel_2011.pdf) | Test repeat-meeting prompts. The experiments support a benefit of interaction in their setting, not a promise that every second meeting will be better. |
| **Contact recency is associated with emotional closeness.** In a study of 251 women's networks, closer relationships generally had more recent contact. The results suggest different maintenance costs for kin and friends; this study does not establish a causal decay rate. | Roberts, S. G. B., & Dunbar, R. I. M. (2011). Communication in social networks: Effects of kinship, network size, and emotional closeness. *Personal Relationships, 18*(3), 439–452. [doi:10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01310.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01310.x) | Test a meeting cadence and optional reminders. The **21-day target is a product hypothesis**, not a deadline established by this study. |

### 2. Fear and miscalibration (why people don't approach)

| Finding | Source | Implication for Firefly Social |
|---------|--------|----------------------|
| **The liking gap:** after a conversation, people consistently underestimate how much their partner liked them. It persists for months among new dorm-mates. Shy people show a bigger gap. | Boothby, E. J., Cooney, G., Sandstrom, G. M., & Clark, M. S. (2018). The liking gap in conversations. *Psychological Science, 29*(11), 1742–1756. [doi:10.1177/0956797618783714](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618783714) · [Yale lab summary](https://clarkrelationshiplab.yale.edu/news/following-conversations-people-believe-they-others-more-others-them) | People won't pick connections they think didn't like them. **Show this finding during the "who did you click with?" step** ("People usually underestimate how much others enjoyed talking to them"). A **mutual, private** opt-in may reduce explicit rejection; its effectiveness needs testing. |
| **People underestimate how pleasant talking to strangers is.** Commuters expected solitude to be nicer, but those told to talk to a stranger reported a more positive experience. A separate waiting-room experiment found benefits for both people in the conversation. | Epley, N., & Schroeder, J. (2014). Mistakenly seeking solitude. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 143*(5), 1980–1999. [doi:10.1037/a0037323](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037323) | Pessimistic forecasts can be one barrier; practical and safety barriers also remain. Firefly Social's job is to **lower the perceived risk of saying hello**, which is what visible intent does. |
| **A week of practice reduces fear.** A scavenger-hunt game of talking to strangers made people's expectations more accurate, with effects measured at follow-up. The intervention is evidence about expectations, not a measured friendship conversion rate. | Sandstrom, G. M., Boothby, E. J., & Cooney, G. (2022). Talking to strangers: A week-long intervention reduces psychological barriers to social connection. *JESP, 102*, 104356. [doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2022.104356](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2022.104356) · [SPSP blog](https://spsp.org/news/character-and-context-blog/sandstrom-boothby-cooney-talking-to-strangers) | Test optional **lightweight missions** at events (e.g. "find someone who's also new to Vancouver"). Their effect in Firefly pods remains untested. |
| **Deep conversations go better than expected.** Strangers overestimated how awkward deep questions would be and underestimated how connected they'd feel. | Kardas, M., Kumar, A., & Epley, N. (2022). Overly shallow? Miscalibrated expectations create a barrier to deeper conversation. *JPSP, 122*(3), 367–398. [doi:10.1037/pspa0000281](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000281) | Conversation openers should go **beyond "what do you do?"**, with one deeper prompt per pod. |
| **Reaching out is appreciated more than we think.** People sending a short check-in message underestimated how much the recipient valued it, with evidence that surprise helps explain the effect. | Liu, P. J., Rim, S., Min, L., & Min, K. E. (2023). The surprise of reaching out: Appreciated more than we think. *JPSP, 124*(4), 754–771. [doi:10.1037/pspi0000402](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000402) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35816566/) | Post-match nudges ("send Priya a quick message") are worth it. Show users this finding to beat the "I don't want to bother them" hesitation. |

### 3. Closeness and similarity

| Finding | Source | Implication for Firefly Social |
|---------|--------|----------------------|
| **Escalating, reciprocal self-disclosure builds closeness quickly.** In Study 1, a 45-minute structured self-disclosure procedure produced greater immediate reported closeness than small talk; it did not establish lasting friendship. | Aron, A., Melinat, E., Aron, E. N., Vallone, R. D., & Bator, R. J. (1997). The experimental generation of interpersonal closeness. *PSPB, 23*(4), 363–377. [doi:10.1177/0146167297234003](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167297234003) | Conversation prompts should **escalate across meetings** (light at meeting 1, deeper by meeting 3). Don't front-load intimacy. |
| **Taking turns disclosing** leads to more liking than one person disclosing while the other listens. | Sprecher, S., Treger, S., Wondra, J. D., Hilaire, N., & Wallpe, K. (2013). Taking turns: Reciprocal self-disclosure promotes liking in initial interactions. *JESP, 49*(5), 860–866. [doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2013.03.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2013.03.017) | Prompts should be **"both answer"** questions, not interview-style. |
| **Actual and perceived similarity both predict attraction overall.** Actual similarity predicted attraction in no-interaction and short-interaction studies, but its effect was not significant in existing relationships. Perceived similarity predicted attraction across all three contexts. | Montoya, R. M., Horton, R. S., & Kirchner, J. (2008). Is actual similarity necessary for attraction? A meta-analysis of actual and perceived similarity. *JSPR, 25*(6), 889–922. [doi:10.1177/0265407508096700](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407508096700) | Don't over-engineer matching. Interests are a **conversation starter**, not a compatibility score. Compare discovered common ground with profile-based matching in a pilot; the meta-analysis did not test app matching algorithms. |

### 4. Groups

| Finding | Source | Implication for Firefly Social |
|---------|--------|----------------------|
| **Spontaneous conversation often involves about four people or fewer.** Observations suggest a practical limit around four, not a universal rule for every structured group. | Dunbar, R. I. M., Duncan, N. D. C., & Nettle, D. (1995). Size and structure of freely forming conversational groups. *Human Nature, 6*(1), 67–78. [doi:10.1007/BF02734136](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02734136) | **Pods of 3–5** at events. Test a target of four; larger pods may split into conversations. |
| **Friendship networks are layered** (~5 intimate, ~15 close, ~50 good friends, ~150 meaningful contacts), as approximate, nested layers, with more contact per relationship in the closer layers. These are population patterns, not fixed individual capacities. | Dunbar, R. I. M. (2018). The anatomy of friendship. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 22*(1), 32–51. [doi:10.1016/j.tics.2017.10.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2017.10.004) | Users have limited capacity. Don't push 20 new connections; aim for **2–5 people who keep meeting**. |
| **Weak ties matter.** On days people interacted with more casual acquaintances, they reported more happiness and belonging. | Sandstrom, G. M., & Dunn, E. W. (2014). Social interactions and well-being: The surprising power of weak ties. *PSPB, 40*(7), 910–922. [doi:10.1177/0146167214529799](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167214529799) | Not every connection must become a close friend. **Being a familiar face at a recurring event** is valuable in itself. Count it as success. |

| Finding | Source | Implication for Firefly Social |
|---------|--------|----------------------|
| Stronger social relationships were associated with higher odds of survival (pooled OR 1.50) across 148 studies and 308,849 participants. This is observational evidence about social relationships, not proof that using a friendship app improves survival. | Holt-Lunstad, J., Smith, T. B., & Layton, J. B. (2010). Social relationships and mortality risk: A meta-analytic review. *PLoS Medicine, 7*(7), e1000316. [doi:10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316) | Grounds the mission. Useful for partners (health, city, university). |
| US Surgeon General declared loneliness a public health epidemic and called for building social infrastructure. | Office of the U.S. Surgeon General (2023). *Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation.* [hhs.gov PDF](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) | Framing for grants and partners. |
| **Metro Vancouver:** 14% reported being often or almost always lonely in 2017, rising to **30% of 18–24 year-olds**. **About half find it difficult to make friends.** Community engagement fell from 2012 to 2017. | Vancouver Foundation (2017). *Connect & Engage* (n = 3,785). [2017 report PDF](https://www.vancouverfoundation.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/VF-Connect-Engage-report.pdf.pdf) · [Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/3888949/new-report-finds-metro-vancouver-is-a-lonely-place-for-young-people) | Hard numbers for the "Vancouver Freeze" in [problem.md](problem.md). ## Design principles derived from the research

1. **Optimize for repeat meetings, not first meetings.** Hours together is the currency (Hall, 2019).
2. **Same people, same place, on a schedule.** Proximity and exposure can support familiarity (Festinger 1950; Moreland & Beach 1992; Back 2008).
3. **Reduce explicit rejection.** Use private, mutual opt-in, and tell users about the liking gap (Boothby 2018; Epley & Schroeder 2014).
4. **Small groups of ~4.** (Dunbar 1995)
5. **Escalating, reciprocal prompts.** (Aron 1997; Sprecher 2013; Kardas 2022)
6. **Use interests to open conversations without treating similarity as destiny.** (Montoya 2008)
7. **Test reminders to maintain contact.** (Roberts & Dunbar 2011; Liu 2023)

## Target metrics suggested by the research

| Metric | Why |
|--------|-----|
| Median hours of shared events per mutual connection at 30 / 90 days | Hall: ~50 h to casual friend |
| % of mutual connections that meet again within 21 days | Pilot target for repeat contact; 21 days is not a research-derived threshold |
| % of users who attend the same recurring series 3+ times | Mere exposure / proximity |
| Self-reported loneliness (consistent wording and response options) at signup vs. 90 days | Descriptive outcome; an opt-in app sample and uncontrolled before/after change are not directly comparable to a weighted national survey or proof of impact |

## Leads still to look into

- Hall (2019) follow-up work on *what activities* count most (play and joking vs. talk).
- Research on **newcomer and immigrant** friendship formation in Canadian cities. (Started: recent immigrants were twice as likely to report virtual-only contact with friends in the preceding month, per [StatCan 2025](interviews/statcan-friendship-surveys.md).)
- Evidence on **gender differences** in approaching strangers and safety concerns.
- **Third places** (Oldenburg, *The Great Good Place*, 1989) and how venue design affects mixing.
